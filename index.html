Tokyo · October 5 and 6

Tokyo, October 5 and 6.

Monday the 5th starts at 5:30 in the morning at the tuna auction, so the morning is already spoken for. You sleep from about half past eight, come back for lunch at noon, and the afternoon starts after that. Tuesday the 6th has nothing fixed before it — the start time is yours. Three choices below: a place for lunch on the 5th, the afternoon that goes with it, and a course for the 6th, or say you’d rather rest.

Monday, October 5 — lunch at noon Pick one. All six are open for lunch on Monday the 5th and take bookings. Each one is the quiet kind, a basement, an upper floor or a back lane, and each lunch is a short course, nowhere near 25 pieces. Five are in or next to Ginza, one is across town.

The smallest option is written on each card. Prices are per person and were checked on each restaurant’s own page on September 27. Travel times are from your hotel.

1 · Higashi-Ginza, behind the Kabuki-za · sushiGinza SushimasaSushi again, but a short one. A basement room on the back side of the Kabuki-za theatre, so the street outside is quiet even at noon. The lunch is a short kaiseki: a few cooked dishes first, then a small round of nigiri, then dessert.

Iro course: 5 small dishes and 7 pieces of nigiri, ¥9,900. Yui course: 8 dishes and 9 pieces, ¥13,200. If you only want nigiri, the Ajisai lunch is 7 pieces with a small bowl of chirashi, ¥4,950.

About 15 min on foot, 5 by taxi. Monday lunch 11:30–14:30. Closed Wednesdays. Booking online or by phone. I book it. Afternoon: course G.

2 · Ginza 5-chome, 9th floor · tempuraTempura KondoTwo Michelin stars, on the ninth floor of a narrow building with almost no sign at street level. You sit at the counter and each piece is fried in front of you and put on your plate one at a time. Vegetables are the speciality here, not just seafood.

Sumire lunch: 9 pieces (2 prawn, 3 fish, 4 vegetable) with rice, pickles, red miso soup and fruit, ¥13,200. The next size up is 11 pieces, ¥16,500. Nothing bigger at lunch.

About 10 min on foot. Monday lunch in two seatings, 12:00 or 13:30. Closed Sundays. Booking by phone or online. I book the 12:00 seating. Afternoon: course G.

3 · Ginza 2-chome, basement · seasonal JapaneseGinza UchiyamaA basement room on a side street at the quiet end of Ginza, booking only, 25 seats with 9 at the counter and two private rooms. The house dish is sea bream over rice with hot tea poured on at the end. The lunch is small plates, one after another, and ends with that.

Lunch course: 7 dishes ending with the sea-bream rice, ¥5,500 with tax and service. An 8-dish version with better ingredients is a little more.

About 20 min on foot, 7 by taxi. Monday lunch 11:30–14:30. Closed on public holidays only (the 5th is not one). Booking online. I book it. Afternoon: course G.

4 · Nihonbashi, 3 min from Tokyo Station · kappoNihonbashi YukariA family restaurant since 1935, now run by the third generation, Kimio Nonaga, who won the Iron Chef title in 2002. Old-style Tokyo cooking, plainly presented. There are private rooms in the basement with sunken tables, so you can have the room to yourselves.

Lunch is a set tray, the Yukari gozen, about ¥4,000, booked the day before. Courses above that on request. All of it is modest in size.

About 10 min by taxi. Monday lunch 11:30–14:00, last order 13:30. Closed Sundays and holidays. Phone booking. I call and book a private room. Afternoon: course G.

5 · Kagurazaka, in the old geisha quarter · seasonal Japanese and udonKagurazaka KurobateiThe one that is not in Ginza. Kagurazaka is a hill of stone-paved lanes that used to be a geisha district, and this restaurant sits in one of the lanes off the main slope. Five private rooms, 30 seats in all. The meal is a tray of small seasonal things with thin hand-cut udon noodles as the main dish.

San-no-zen lunch tray: an appetiser plate, sashimi, a seasonal dish, tempura, udon and a sweet, ¥5,000 with tax and service. One tray each, nothing else arrives.

About 20 min by taxi. Monday lunch 11:30–14:30, last order 14:00. Closed Sundays. Booking online. I book a private room. Afternoon: course H.

Monday, October 5 — the afternoon The afternoon follows the lunch, on foot, so nobody crosses the city twice. Lunch 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 goes with course G in Ginza; lunch 5 goes with course H in Kagurazaka. You will have been awake since 4:15, so both end by five and the evening stays empty.

Course G · Stationery, a food hall, a rooftop garden, tea · $250 · about four hours

Ginza, on foot from the table

This goes with lunch 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6. You stand up from the table and the afternoon is already around you: everything below is inside one neighbourhood, flat, and about two kilometres end to end, with a seat every forty minutes. It is the shopping afternoon — paper, pens, a food hall, sweets to carry back — with a garden on a roof in the middle of it. From lunch 4 in Nihonbashi it starts with a ten-minute taxi; from the others you walk.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 13:30
    Itoya, Ginza 2-chomeTwelve floors of paper and pens in one narrow building, since 1904. Letter sets, washi paper, fountain pens, wrapping — gifts that pack flat. There is a café on the top floor if you want to sit before you start.
  2. 14:30
    Mitsukoshi, the basement food hallFive minutes down the main street. Two floors under the department store of wagashi, tea, pickles, rice crackers and bento from every part of Japan, most of it boxed to travel. The easiest place in Tokyo to buy food gifts, and I translate the counters.
  3. 15:15
    Kimuraya, across the crossingThe bakery that invented the sweet-bean bun in 1874, on the corner of the Ginza crossing under the clock tower. A paper bag of five for the hotel room.
  4. 15:30
    Ginza Six, the roofA department store with a garden on top, free, open to anyone. Benches, trees, and the whole of Ginza below you. Then the art bookshop on the sixth floor, which is worth a look even if you buy nothing.
  5. 16:20
    Tea at Nakamura TokichiOn the fourth floor of the same building: a tea house from Uji, near Kyoto, that has been selling matcha since 1854. A bowl of tea, or a matcha jelly and ice cream, sitting down. This is the rest before the walk home.
  6. 17:00
    Back at the hotelAbout twelve minutes on foot, or a five-minute taxi.

The route

Lunch → Itoya on foot (5–15 min, or a 10-min taxi from Nihonbashi). Itoya → Mitsukoshi 5 min. Mitsukoshi → Kimuraya across the crossing. Kimuraya → Ginza Six 5 min. Ginza Six → hotel about 12 min on foot or a short taxi. Open the route in Google Maps ↗

Course H · Old geisha lanes, a glass shrine, sweets and rice · $250 · about four hours

Kagurazaka, the hill after lunch

This goes with lunch 5. Kagurazaka is a hill of stone-paved lanes that was a geisha district a hundred years ago and is still the quietest old quarter in central Tokyo. The restaurant is near the top, so the afternoon is a slow walk down it: a sweet shop, a shrine rebuilt in glass and wood, a rice-and-kitchen shop for gifts, a red temple gate, then the lanes. You finish at the station at the bottom and one train takes you to your hotel.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 13:30
    Baikatei, three minutes from lunchA wagashi shop since 1935 with a counter of seasonal sweets and the shop’s own hand-shaped “ukishima” cakes. A box for the hotel, or one each now.
  2. 13:45
    Akagi Shrine, and a coffee on its stepsA shrine at the top of the hill, rebuilt in 2010 by the architect Kengo Kuma in glass and cedar. Unusual, quiet, and with a café on the grounds where we sit for half an hour. Open Monday.
  3. 14:30
    Akomeya, in la kaguAcross the road: a rice shop that grew into a whole floor of kitchen things — rice from single farms, bowls, knives, tea, packaged food gifts — in a converted warehouse. This is the gift stop of the day. Open Monday 11:00–20:00.
  4. 15:20
    Zenkoku-ji, the red gateFive minutes down the main street. A small temple with a bright red gate and a pair of stone tigers, here since 1792. Two minutes to look, a bench in the courtyard if you want it.
  5. 15:35
    The lanesBehind the temple: Hyogo-yokocho and Kakurenbo-yokocho, stone-paved alleys between black wooden fences where the geisha houses were. Flat, short, and the reason people come here. We take it slowly.
  6. 16:15
    Down to Iidabashi, and homeThe bottom of the hill is Iidabashi station. One train, about 15 minutes, to Yurakucho, which is 8 minutes on foot from your hotel — or a taxi the whole way, about 20 minutes.
  7. 16:45
    Back at the hotel

The route

All on foot, about 1.5 km and downhill. Iidabashi → Yurakucho 15 min by train, then 8 min on foot, or a 20-min taxi door to door. Open the route in Google Maps ↗

Course D · National museum, kaiseki lunch, lotus pond · $250 · about four hours

Ueno, the museum and the pond

Tuesday is entirely your call on timing, so this course is written for a late-morning start with no auction behind it. One museum building, a sit-down lunch, and a pond to finish — indoors-heavy, with benches in nearly every room and a free wheelchair on loan at the entrance if you want one.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Start from the hotel, whenever you likeAbout 25 minutes by train to Ueno. This time is yours to move.
  2. 10:30
    Tokyo National Museum, HonkanJust the main building — the Honkan — not the whole museum complex, which would be a full day on its own. Japanese sculpture, swords and screens in order from ancient to Edo. Benches in most rooms, elevators between floors, and a wheelchair free to borrow at the entrance if you want to save your legs.
  3. 12:15
    Lunch at Innsyoutei, inside the parkA kaiseki restaurant since 1875, built like an old Japanese house with huge windows onto the trees. Tempura and vegetable-forward set lunches, seated, no rush.
  4. 13:45
    Shinobazu PondA lotus pond a few minutes’ walk from the restaurant, with a small island shrine and rowboats for hire. We just sit by the water; the boats are there if you want them.
  5. 14:30
    Back at the hotelAbout 25 minutes by train.

The route

Course E · Edo castle grounds, brick Tokyo, coffee · $250 · about four hours

The East Gardens, and Marunouchi

This is the one that barely leaves the neighbourhood. The Imperial Palace East Gardens are a ten-minute walk from your hotel, free to enter, and closed only on Mondays and Fridays — Tuesday is a normal open day. After that, a coffee in a restored 1894 brick building and a stop at Tokyo Station on the way back, all still within about fifteen minutes of your door.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Walk to the East GardensAbout 10 minutes on foot from the hotel to the Otemon gate.
  2. 10:15
    Otemon and the castle groundsThe main gate of Edo Castle, and inside it the grounds where the shogun’s government once stood — now lawns, stone walls and a guardhouse. Flat paths throughout.
  3. 11:00
    The keep foundationThe stone base of what was once Japan’s tallest castle keep, burned down in 1657 and never rebuilt. You can climb onto the top of it — a short flight of stone steps — for a view over the whole garden and a bench to rest on.
  4. 11:45
    Coffee, MarunouchiA 10–15 minute walk to Café 1894, inside the Mitsubishi Ichigokan Museum — a restored 1894 red-brick bank building with a two-storey glass-roofed hall.
  5. 13:00
    Tokyo Station, IchibangaiA short walk to the underground arcade beneath the station — a food hall and souvenir street if you want to pick up gifts before you leave the area.
  6. 13:45
    Back at the hotelAbout 15 minutes on foot, or a short taxi.

The route

Course F · Kitchen street, food samples, Senso-ji · $250 · about five and a half hours

Kappabashi, and the old temple next door

For the two of you who like a kitchen and a gift shop. Kappabashi is Tokyo's restaurant-supply street — knives, tableware, and the shops that make the plastic food models you have seen in every restaurant window. Then a soba lunch, and Tokyo’s oldest temple ten minutes away on foot, with a three-hundred-year-old street of stalls in front of it, before a taxi home.

Guide fee $250 · about five and a half hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Start from the hotelAbout 20 minutes by train or taxi to Kappabashi, in Asakusa.
  2. 10:30
    Kappabashi Kitchen StreetA few hundred metres of restaurant-supply shops: knives that can be engraved with your name while you wait, lacquerware, and the original workshops behind Japan’s plastic food displays. Most shops are open Tuesdays — it is Sundays that are quiet here.
  3. 11:15
    Make your own food sampleAt Ganso Shokuhin Sample-ya, the shop that popularised the craft in 1932. A booked 40-minute session making a piece of wax or plastic tempura or lettuce, which you keep. I book the slot in advance.
  4. 12:15
    Lunch, Namiki YabusobaA soba restaurant near Kaminarimon since 1913, a few minutes’ walk from Kappabashi. Seated, quick, and open Tuesdays.
  5. 13:30
    Senso-ji, on footTen minutes from the soba shop. Tokyo’s oldest temple, founded in 628: in under the five-metre paper lantern, along Nakamise — a street of rice-cracker and fan stalls that has been a shopping street for three hundred years — to the main hall. Flat, with benches in the courtyard.
  6. 15:00
    Something sweet on NakamiseMelon bread out of the oven, or a bag of hot senbei, eaten as we walk back.
  7. 15:30
    Taxi to the hotelAbout 25 minutes across the city, sitting down.

The route

What it costs The same basis both days.

US$250 a day — or ¥40,000 in cash — whichever course you pick.

Four hours or five, it is the same figure — so pick the one you actually want rather than the one that looks like less work.

The fee covers my time only, set up the way we agreed: entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come. Dollars by payment link, like the kabuki tickets, or yen in cash on the day — whichever is easier for you. Nothing is owed if you wake up and decide you would rather not.

And one honest word.

If you wake up on the 5th, do the auction, sleep, and then decide that the afternoon is more than you want, say so and we cancel it. Nothing is owed. That is a good answer and I would rather have it than watch you push through.

Reply to Yuuki with a lunch number and a letter for each day — or with “none, we will rest”, which is a real answer and not a disappointing one. Times are approximate and can move earlier or later on the day.
