Tokyo · October 5 and 6

Tokyo, October 5 and 6.

Monday the 5th starts at 5:30 in the morning at the tuna auction, so the morning is already spoken for. You sleep from about half past eight, come back for lunch in Hibiya at noon, and the afternoon starts after that. Tuesday the 6th has nothing fixed before it — the start time is yours. Three choices below: a place for lunch on the 5th, then a course for each afternoon, or say you’d rather rest.

Monday, October 5 — lunch at noon Pick one. All five are open for lunch on Monday the 5th, take bookings, and are within ten minutes of your hotel.

You said the sushi on the 20th was too much food. Each place below lets you decide how much arrives, and the smallest option is written on each card. Numbers 2 and 4 are in the same brick arches under the train line as the sushi place; the other three are a few minutes away on foot. Prices are per person and were checked on each restaurant’s own page on September 25.

1 · Imperial Hotel, lower level · sushiSushi NakataThe same kind of lunch as the 20th, at about a third of the size. A quiet counter in the basement of the Imperial Hotel, with private rooms if you would rather have a table to yourselves.

Smallest set: 7 pieces of nigiri, a small starter, omelet and soup. ¥4,600. There is an 8-piece set at ¥6,500 if you want a little more.

About 5 min on foot. Monday lunch 11:30–15:00. Closed Sundays only. Phone booking. I call and book it.

2 · Hibiya OKUROJI · sobaSobamae IsshinBuckwheat noodles made from 100% buckwheat flour, in the same brick arches under the train line as the 20th. You eat in separate private rooms, so it is the quietest of the five, and it is also the lightest meal.

You order one bowl or tray, from plain cold soba (¥900) up to duck soba or prawn tempura soba (¥1,500–2,000). Nothing else arrives unless you ask for it.

About 3 min on foot. Lunch 11:30–14:30, every day. Booking by phone or online. I book the room.

3 · Tokyo Midtown Hibiya, 3rd floor · soba and seasonal JapaneseHibiya RokusanteiA new restaurant, opened on September 11 by the team of Rokusaburo Michiba, one of the original Iron Chefs. 25 seats, two private rooms, and an English menu. Soba is the main dish, with small seasonal plates around it.

Lunch from ¥1,980 for a soba set, up to ¥5,500 for a short course. You choose how far up the menu to go.

About 10 min on foot. Monday lunch 11:00–15:00. Closed Wednesdays. Booking by phone. I book it.

4 · Hibiya OKUROJI · charcoal-grilled eelSumiyaki UnafujiAn eel restaurant from Nagoya, listed in the Michelin Bib Gourmand, in the same arches. The rooms are calm and plain, with four semi-private rooms. Hitsumabushi is the dish: grilled eel over rice that you eat three ways, the last one with broth poured over.

Smallest: a bowl with 5/6 of an eel, clam soup and pickles, about ¥5,900. A half-size eel set with small side dishes is about ¥7,300.

About 3 min on foot. Open 11:00–22:00 without a break. Booking online or by phone. I book it.

5 · Tokyo Midtown Hibiya, 3rd floor · Kyoto kaisekiNanzenji HyoteiThe Tokyo branch of a Kyoto restaurant that has been serving travellers by the Nanzenji temple for about 400 years. 24 seats and a private room. The most formal and the most expensive of the five.

In October the lighter lunch is sea bream over rice with hot tea poured on, at ¥10,890. The full kaiseki is ¥14,520. Both prices include tax and service.

About 10 min on foot. Monday lunch 12:00–15:00, last order 13:30. Closed Wednesdays and the 1st and 3rd Tuesday. Booking required. I book it.

Course A · Old temple, river boat, garden · $250 · about five hours

Asakusa, and the river home

You will have been awake since a quarter past four and had lunch already in Hibiya. This course is built so that the moving after that is done sitting down: a temple in the morning district, then a boat that carries you back down the Sumida and drops you almost at your door. Nothing here needs booking and nothing here is far.

Guide fee $250 · about five hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 13:15
    After lunch in HibiyaLunch finishes a few minutes from the hotel. About 25 minutes by train from there to Asakusa. You can sleep on it.
  2. 13:45
    Senso-ji, AsakusaTokyo’s oldest temple, founded in 628. You come in under a five-metre paper lantern, down a street of stalls selling rice crackers and fans that has been a shopping street for three hundred years. Flat the whole way, benches in the courtyard.
  3. 15:00
    Something sweet on NakamiseMelon bread straight out of the oven, or a bag of hot senbei. We eat as we walk back to the pier.
  4. 15:40
    The boat down the SumidaForty minutes on the water. Twelve bridges, each one a different colour, the Skytree behind you and the city sliding past. You are sitting the whole way and there is a toilet on board.
  5. 16:30
    Hama-rikyu GardensThe boat lands inside a 400-year-old shogun’s garden with saltwater ponds. Flat gravel paths, benches all the way round, the skyline standing over the pines.
  6. 17:15
    Tea over the pond, if you want itA tea house on stilts out over the water. Matcha whisked in front of you with a seasonal sweet. Entirely optional.
  7. 18:00
    Back at the hotelFifteen minutes on foot, or one short taxi.

The route

Hotel → Asakusa about 25 min by train. Temple on foot. Pier is 5 min from the temple. Boat to Hama-rikyu 40 min. Garden → hotel 15 min on foot. Open the route in Google Maps ↗

Course B · Dark rooms, water, light · $250 · about four hours

teamLab, and almost no walking

If the market has finished you off, this is the course that asks least of your legs. It is one building, indoors, dark and cool, and you are back at the hotel before dinner. It is also in Toyosu — the same direction you will already have travelled that morning, so nothing about the trains is new.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 13:30
    After lunch in HibiyaAbout 20 minutes by train to Toyosu, the same line you took at dawn.
  2. 14:00
    teamLab Planets, ToyosuYou take your shoes off at the door and walk through the work barefoot. One room is ankle-deep warm water with projected koi that scatter when you move; another is a mirrored hall of hanging lights; another is a floor of orchids overhead. It is about an hour and a half at a slow pace.
  3. 15:45
    Coffee and a sit downThere is a garden and a tea room in the same building.
  4. 16:30
    Toyosu Senkyaku BanraiTen minutes on foot. A wooden market-town building beside the fish market with food stalls on two floors and a rooftop footbath looking over the bay. Free, and you can sit with your feet in hot water and do nothing.
  5. 17:30
    Back at the hotelAbout 20 minutes.

The route

Course C · Forest shrine, Harajuku, Shibuya · $250 · about five hours

Meiji shrine and the young side of Tokyo

The version for the two of you who wake up feeling fine. A forest shrine in the middle of the city, then the loudest teenage street in Japan ten minutes away, then the crossing everybody has seen on television. It is the most walking of the three courses and the most Tokyo.

Guide fee $250 · about five hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 13:15
    After lunch in HibiyaAbout 20 minutes by train from Hibiya to Harajuku.
  2. 13:45
    Meiji JinguA wide gravel path through a forest of a hundred thousand trees, every one donated and planted by hand in 1920. Benches the whole way. On a weekday afternoon it is quiet enough to hear the gravel.
  3. 14:45
    Takeshita StreetTen minutes and a complete change of world: three hundred metres of teenage fashion, crepe stands and rainbow cotton candy. We walk it once, which is enough.
  4. 15:30
    OmotesandoThe wide tree-lined avenue below it, calmer, with the architecture worth looking up at. Coffee sitting down.
  5. 16:45
    ShibuyaThe crossing from above first, through the window of the station building, then down into it. We stop the moment you have had enough.
  6. 18:00
    Back at the hotelAbout 20 minutes by train.

The route

Hotel → Harajuku about 20 min by train. Shrine walk 10 min each way. Shrine → Takeshita 10 min on foot. Takeshita → Omotesando 8 min. Omotesando → Shibuya 12 min on foot or 3 min by train. Shibuya → hotel about 20 min. Open the route in Google Maps ↗

Course D · National museum, kaiseki lunch, lotus pond · $250 · about four hours

Ueno, the museum and the pond

Tuesday is entirely your call on timing, so this course is written for a late-morning start with no auction behind it. One museum building, a sit-down lunch, and a pond to finish — indoors-heavy, with benches in nearly every room and a free wheelchair on loan at the entrance if you want one.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Start from the hotel, whenever you likeAbout 25 minutes by train to Ueno. This time is yours to move.
  2. 10:30
    Tokyo National Museum, HonkanJust the main building — the Honkan — not the whole museum complex, which would be a full day on its own. Japanese sculpture, swords and screens in order from ancient to Edo. Benches in most rooms, elevators between floors, and a wheelchair free to borrow at the entrance if you want to save your legs.
  3. 12:15
    Lunch at Innsyoutei, inside the parkA kaiseki restaurant since 1875, built like an old Japanese house with huge windows onto the trees. Tempura and vegetable-forward set lunches, seated, no rush.
  4. 13:45
    Shinobazu PondA lotus pond a few minutes’ walk from the restaurant, with a small island shrine and rowboats for hire. We just sit by the water; the boats are there if you want them.
  5. 14:30
    Back at the hotelAbout 25 minutes by train.

The route

Course E · Edo castle grounds, brick Tokyo, coffee · $250 · about four hours

The East Gardens, and Marunouchi

This is the one that barely leaves the neighbourhood. The Imperial Palace East Gardens are a ten-minute walk from your hotel, free to enter, and closed only on Mondays and Fridays — Tuesday is a normal open day. After that, a coffee in a restored 1894 brick building and a stop at Tokyo Station on the way back, all still within about fifteen minutes of your door.

Guide fee $250 · about four hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Walk to the East GardensAbout 10 minutes on foot from the hotel to the Otemon gate.
  2. 10:15
    Otemon and the castle groundsThe main gate of Edo Castle, and inside it the grounds where the shogun’s government once stood — now lawns, stone walls and a guardhouse. Flat paths throughout.
  3. 11:00
    The keep foundationThe stone base of what was once Japan’s tallest castle keep, burned down in 1657 and never rebuilt. You can climb onto the top of it — a short flight of stone steps — for a view over the whole garden and a bench to rest on.
  4. 11:45
    Coffee, MarunouchiA 10–15 minute walk to Café 1894, inside the Mitsubishi Ichigokan Museum — a restored 1894 red-brick bank building with a two-storey glass-roofed hall.
  5. 13:00
    Tokyo Station, IchibangaiA short walk to the underground arcade beneath the station — a food hall and souvenir street if you want to pick up gifts before you leave the area.
  6. 13:45
    Back at the hotelAbout 15 minutes on foot, or a short taxi.

The route

Course F · Kitchen street, food samples, Ginza shopping · $250 · about five and a half hours

Kappabashi, and Ginza for gifts

For the two of you who like a kitchen and a gift shop more than a shrine. Kappabashi is Tokyo's restaurant-supply street — knives, tableware, and the shops that make the plastic food models you have seen in every restaurant window. Then a soba lunch, and a taxi to Ginza for stationery, a department-store food hall, and whatever else catches your eye, ending a short ride from your hotel.

Guide fee $250 · about five and a half hours. Entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come.

The day

  1. 10:00
    Start from the hotelAbout 20 minutes by train or taxi to Kappabashi, in Asakusa.
  2. 10:30
    Kappabashi Kitchen StreetA few hundred metres of restaurant-supply shops: knives that can be engraved with your name while you wait, lacquerware, and the original workshops behind Japan’s plastic food displays. Most shops are open Tuesdays — it is Sundays that are quiet here.
  3. 11:15
    Make your own food sampleAt Ganso Shokuhin Sample-ya, the shop that popularised the craft in 1932. A booked 40-minute session making a piece of wax or plastic tempura or lettuce, which you keep. I book the slot in advance.
  4. 12:15
    Lunch, Namiki YabusobaA soba restaurant near Kaminarimon since 1913, a few minutes’ walk from Kappabashi. Seated, quick, and open Tuesdays.
  5. 13:30
    Taxi to GinzaAbout 25 minutes by taxi across the city.
  6. 14:00
    GinzaItoya’s twelve floors of stationery, the food hall in the basement of Mitsukoshi, and Akomeya for rice, tea and packaged gifts — as much or as little of it as you want.
  7. 16:00
    Back at the hotelGinza is next to Hibiya — a short walk, or a five-minute taxi.

The route

What it costs The same basis both days.

US$250 a day — or ¥40,000 in cash — whichever course you pick.

Four hours or five, it is the same figure — so pick the one you actually want rather than the one that looks like less work.

The fee covers my time only, set up the way we agreed: entry tickets, trains, taxis and meals are settled on the day as they come. Dollars by payment link, like the kabuki tickets, or yen in cash on the day — whichever is easier for you. Nothing is owed if you wake up and decide you would rather not.

And one honest word.

If you wake up on the 5th, do the auction, sleep, and then decide that the afternoon is more than you want, say so and we cancel it. Nothing is owed. That is a good answer and I would rather have it than watch you push through.
